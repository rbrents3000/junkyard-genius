## 🧪 What Is It?

Every metal produces a characteristic color when heated — it's how astronomers know what distant stars are made of, and it's how fireworks get their colors. Copper compounds burn blue-green. Lithium burns crimson red. Boric acid burns vivid green. Sodium burns bright yellow. Soak pine cones or wood chips in metal salt solutions, dry them out, and toss them into a fire. Each one erupts in its signature color. It's the simplest chemistry demo that exists and it never stops being impressive.

<details>
<summary><strong>🧰 Ingredients</strong></summary>

- [ ] Pine cones or small wood chunks — the more porous the better *(yard, park, craft store)*
- [ ] Copper chloride or copper sulfate — produces blue-green flames *(hardware store as root killer, or chemistry supplier)*
- [ ] Lithium from old batteries — produces deep red flames *(dead laptop batteries, button cells)*
- [ ] Boric acid — produces green flames *(pharmacy, hardware store as roach killer)*
- [ ] Table salt (sodium chloride) — produces yellow flames *(kitchen)*
- [ ] Strontium chloride — produces bright red flames *(emergency flares, chemistry supplier)*
- [ ] Plastic buckets — one per color *(dollar store)*
- [ ] Water *(tap)*
- [ ] Gloves — nitrile or rubber *(pharmacy)*

</details>

## 🔨 Build Steps

1. **Prepare the salt solutions.** Dissolve each metal salt separately in warm water in its own bucket. Use roughly 1/2 cup of salt per gallon of water. Label each bucket clearly. More salt = more saturated = more vivid color.
2. **Harvest lithium (if using).** Carefully disassemble old lithium batteries outdoors. The lithium metal strip inside is what you want. Dissolve small pieces in water slowly — it will fizz and produce hydrogen gas, so do this outside and away from flames. The resulting lithium hydroxide solution works for soaking.
3. **Soak the pine cones.** Submerge pine cones or wood chunks in each solution for at least 24 hours. Weigh them down with a rock or plate so they stay submerged. Longer soaking means deeper saturation.
4. **Dry completely.** Remove the pine cones and let them dry for 2-3 days on newspaper in a well-ventilated area. They need to be bone dry before burning. Damp pine cones will just steam and sputter.
5. **Label your colors.** Keep each batch separate and labeled. Zip-lock bags work well for storage. You want to know which one is which when you're standing around the fire.
6. **Build your fire.** Get a normal wood fire going with a solid bed of coals. The hotter the fire, the more vivid the colors. A weak fire produces weak colors.
7. **Toss them in.** Drop one pine cone at a time into the fire and watch the color bloom. Space them out for maximum effect. Mixing multiple colors at once creates wild combinations but can muddy into brownish tones.
8. **Photograph it.** The colors show up brilliantly on camera with manual exposure settings. Slightly underexpose to saturate the color. Long exposures capture the shifting hues beautifully.

## ⚠️ Safety Notes

- Copper compounds are toxic. Do not cook food over a fire that has had treated pine cones in it. Use a dedicated fire pit for color demonstrations.
- Lithium metal reacts violently with water and is flammable in air. Disassemble batteries outdoors, one at a time, with gloves and eye protection. Never puncture a battery that is swollen or warm.
- The smoke from burning metal salts can be irritating. Stay upwind and burn in well-ventilated outdoor areas only.
