## 🧪 What Is It?

Water's solvent properties change dramatically with temperature. At room temperature, water is a polar solvent — it dissolves salts, sugars, and polar molecules but ignores fats, oils, waxes, and most organic compounds. That's why oil and water don't mix. But as water temperature rises above its normal boiling point (only possible under pressure, since it would otherwise become steam), its dielectric constant drops. At 250°F under 15 PSI, water's polarity decreases enough that it starts dissolving compounds that cold water can't touch — essential oils, plant waxes, lignin, certain polymers, and a range of organic molecules that normally require alcohol, hexane, or other organic solvents to extract.

This is the principle behind subcritical water extraction, a technique that green chemistry labs use to extract active compounds from plant material without organic solvents. Instead of soaking herbs in ethanol for weeks to make a tincture, you put the plant material in superheated water at 250°F for an hour and the water does the same job. The extracted compounds are dissolved in the water; when you cool the solution, many of them precipitate out or can be separated by filtration. No flammable solvents, no residual toxicity, no waiting.

A pressure cooker at 15 PSI holds water at 250°F — well within the subcritical extraction range. Load plant material and water into the cooker, pressurize, hold, and cool. You've performed a solvent extraction using nothing but water and heat. The extracts are cleaner than alcohol-based tinctures because water at this temperature is selective — it pulls the target compounds without dissolving chlorophyll and other undesirable co-extractants that make alcohol extracts taste like lawn clippings.

## 🔨 Build Steps

1. **Select and prepare plant material.** Dried material extracts more efficiently than fresh because the cell walls are already broken down. Chop, grind, or crush the material to increase surface area — finer particles extract faster. Good candidates for pressure extraction: dried lavender (essential oils), rosemary (antioxidant compounds), turmeric (curcumin), cinnamon bark (cinnamaldehyde), pine needles (vitamin C and terpenes), willow bark (salicin), and dried mushrooms (polysaccharides). Weigh your material — a good starting ratio is 1 part plant material to 10 parts water by weight.

2. **Load the pressure cooker.** Place the plant material directly in the stainless steel pot or inside a mesh bag for easy removal. Add distilled water at the target ratio. Do not fill past the 2/3 line — plant material expands when saturated and you need headspace for pressure. For delicate compounds that degrade at high heat, add a splash of citric acid (lemon juice) to lower the pH — acidic conditions stabilize many antioxidant compounds during extraction.

3. **Pressurize and extract.** Lock the lid and bring to 15 PSI. Reduce heat to maintain steady pressure. Hold for 30–60 minutes depending on the material density and target compound. Soft herbs and flowers extract in 30 minutes. Hard bark, roots, and woody material need the full 60. The superheated water penetrates cell walls, dissolves soluble compounds, and carries them into solution. The liquid will darken significantly as extraction progresses.

4. **Cool and filter.** Turn off the heat and let the cooker depressurize naturally. Slow cooling allows dissolved compounds that are heat-sensitive to gradually transition to lower temperature without shock. Open the lid and strain the liquid through a fine mesh strainer to remove bulk plant material. For a clearer extract, filter again through cheesecloth and then a coffee filter. The resulting liquid is your subcritical water extract.

5. **Concentrate if needed.** The extract is dilute — it's mostly water. To concentrate it, pour into a wide pan and simmer uncovered (at atmospheric pressure now) until reduced by 50–80%. Don't boil vigorously — some extracted compounds are volatile and will evaporate. For heat-sensitive extracts, freeze the liquid and then let it thaw through a filter — the ice crystals that form are mostly pure water, so the liquid that thaws first is more concentrated in extract (freeze concentration).

6. **Store and use.** Store extracts in glass jars in the refrigerator. Most water-based extracts have limited shelf life (1–2 weeks) unless you add a preservative (citric acid, rosemary extract, or 20% alcohol). Uses include: natural dyes (turmeric and bark extracts), flavoring (herb and spice extracts), soap and cosmetic ingredients (lavender and rosemary extracts), and natural cleaning solutions (pine and citrus extracts). Compare the aroma and potency to a cold-water infusion of the same material — the pressure extraction will be dramatically stronger.

## ⚠️ Safety Notes

- Do not pressure-extract material you cannot identify with certainty. Some plants contain toxic compounds that concentrate during extraction. If you're foraging, confirm identification with a field guide or expert.
- Superheated extracts from resinous materials (pine, cedar) can foam violently when pressure is released. Always depressurize slowly and naturally.
- Do not extract materials containing volatile organic solvents or flammable compounds under pressure. This applies to anything pre-soaked in alcohol, turpentine, or petroleum solvents — the combination of heat, pressure, and flammable vapor is an explosion risk.
- Label the cooker "NOT FOR FOOD" if extracting non-food plants or plants treated with pesticides.
