## 🧪 What Is It?

Growing gourmet and medicinal mushrooms — shiitake, lion's mane, oyster, reishi, maitake — starts with sterile substrate. Mushroom mycelium needs a nutrient-rich growing medium (grain, sawdust, straw, or supplemented wood chips) that has been completely sterilized so the only organism that grows in it is the mushroom you inoculated, not the ten thousand species of mold, bacteria, and wild yeast that are floating in every cubic meter of air in your house.

Sterilization means killing everything, including bacterial endospores — the armored dormant forms of bacteria like Bacillus that survive boiling water, UV light, alcohol, and basically everything except sustained heat above 250°F. Boiling water maxes out at 212°F. It pasteurizes (kills vegetative cells) but doesn't sterilize (kill endospores). That's why every mushroom cultivation guide says the same thing: you need a pressure cooker. At 15 PSI, water reaches 250°F — hot enough to denature the proteins that protect endospores. Hold that for 90 minutes and the substrate is genuinely sterile.

This isn't a hack or a clever repurposing — this is the standard, established, universally recommended method for mushroom substrate sterilization. Every mycology forum, every mushroom farming book, every university extension service says the same thing: buy a pressure cooker. The Presto 23-quart canner is called "the mycologist's best friend" in online growing communities because it holds 7 quart jars of grain spawn per batch. The entire home mushroom cultivation industry — worth billions globally — runs on thrift store pressure cookers doing exactly what this build describes.

## 🔨 Build Steps

1. **Prepare the grain.** Soak whole oats or rye berries in water for 12–24 hours. Drain and simmer for 15 minutes until the grains are hydrated but not split or mushy — you want them swollen with moisture but still intact. Dry the surface by spreading on a towel for 30 minutes. The grain should be damp inside but dry on the outside. Too wet and the jars turn into bacterial soup; too dry and the mycelium can't colonize.

2. **Modify the jar lids.** Drill two 1/4" holes in each mason jar lid. Cover one hole from the top with a dab of high-temperature RTV silicone — this is the self-healing injection port where you'll push a syringe needle through to inoculate without opening the jar. Cover the other hole with a square of micropore tape (the medical tape from the pharmacy) — this allows gas exchange (mycelium needs oxygen and produces CO2) while filtering out contaminant spores. The jar stays sealed from the environment but breathes through the filter.

3. **Fill and load the jars.** Fill each jar 2/3 full with prepared grain. Screw on the modified lid firmly. Cover each lid with a square of aluminum foil — this prevents condensation from dripping onto the micropore tape during sterilization, which would block gas exchange. Load jars onto the pressure cooker rack. A 23-quart cooker holds 7 quart jars standing upright. Add 3 inches of water to the bottom.

4. **Sterilize at 15 PSI for 90 minutes.** Lock the lid and bring to full pressure over high heat. Vent steam for 10 minutes to purge air before placing the jiggler weight. Once at 15 PSI, reduce heat to maintain a gentle rock and start the timer. Grain substrate needs 90 full minutes at 15 PSI to ensure complete sterilization — the heat must penetrate to the center of each jar and maintain kill temperature long enough to destroy endospores. Do not shortcut this time. Contamination rates correlate directly with sterilization time — 60 minutes gives you 70% success, 90 minutes gives you 95%+.

5. **Cool undisturbed for 12 hours.** Turn off the heat and leave the cooker completely alone. Do not open it. Do not vent it. Let it cool to room temperature naturally — this takes 8–12 hours (overnight is ideal). The jars are sterile inside. Every time you open the cooker before it's fully cooled, you expose still-warm jars to room air. Warm condensation on jar lids pulls in airborne contaminant spores through the micropore tape by capillary action. Patience here directly determines your contamination rate.

6. **Inoculate in a still air box.** Build a still air box from a clear plastic tote with two arm holes cut in one side. Wipe the interior with isopropyl alcohol. Place cooled jars and your spore syringe inside. Flame-sterilize the syringe needle with a lighter until red-hot. Push the needle through the RTV injection port and inject 1–2 mL of spore solution against the glass wall where you can see it. The silicone self-heals when you withdraw the needle — no contaminants enter.

7. **Incubate and colonize.** Store inoculated jars in a warm, dark place (75–80°F is ideal for most species). Within 3–7 days, you'll see white fuzzy mycelium growing from the inoculation point. Over 2–4 weeks, the mycelium colonizes the entire jar, consuming the grain as food. A fully colonized jar is solid white with no visible grain — it looks like a jar of white felt. Any spots of green, black, orange, or pink are contaminants; discard those jars outdoors without opening them.

8. **Spawn to bulk substrate.** Once jars are fully colonized, break up the colonized grain and mix it into a pasteurized bulk substrate — straw, coco coir, hardwood sawdust, or supplemented wood chips depending on the mushroom species. The colonized grain acts as a seed, spreading mycelium rapidly through the bulk substrate. Within 1–2 weeks, the substrate is fully colonized and ready to fruit. Introduce fresh air, light, and humidity, and mushrooms appear within days. A single pressure cooker batch of 7 jars can inoculate 50–100 pounds of bulk substrate — enough for dozens of harvests.

## ⚠️ Safety Notes

- This build is for growing gourmet and medicinal mushroom species (shiitake, oyster, lion's mane, reishi, etc.). Do not attempt to cultivate wild mushrooms from unidentified spores — some mushroom species produce deadly toxins.
- Standard pressure cooker safety applies throughout. Never force the lid, always wait for full depressurization, inspect the gasket before each use.
- Grain substrate left at room temperature without sterilization can grow dangerous mold. If a jar smells foul or shows colored mold growth, do not open it indoors. Dispose outdoors.
- The pressure cooker used for substrate sterilization can safely be returned to food use after thorough washing — grain substrate is food-grade material. This is one of the few builds in this category where the cooker doesn't need to be permanently retired from food duty.
